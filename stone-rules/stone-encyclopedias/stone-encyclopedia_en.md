# Stone Encyclopedia

A detailed guide to ten rock types for a home collection and the Stone Shelter project.

## About This Guide

The geological origins, structures, and basic properties reflect the real characteristics of these rocks. Recommendations for placing small, untreated specimens around the home are practical interpretations of those properties, rather than instructions for maintaining architectural stone. An individual specimen's behavior depends on its cracks, weathering, porosity, and surface treatment.

**Psychological vibes, personalities, preferences, and rituals are fictional.** The stones are presented as pets, each with a temperament, a favorite spot, and a few domestic quirks. These descriptions do not claim healing or mystical effects.

None of these rocks needs light, watering, or daily feeding. “Likes a windowsill” means that it can be displayed there conveniently and attractively; “prefers a dark corner” describes a fictional personality. Care means removing dust, providing suitable storage conditions, and checking the specimen's condition.

## Rating Scales

All ratings below are approximate categories for a home stone catalog, rather than laboratory measurements.

### Fragility Scale

Fragility describes the risk of chipping, splitting, delamination, or crumbling during ordinary handling. It differs from hardness, which measures resistance to scratching. Even a hard stone can break when dropped.

| Score | Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1/5 | Very low | A solid, intact specimen is relatively difficult to damage with careful handling. |
| 2/5 | Low | Generally sturdy; an edge impact, an existing crack, or a fall onto tile may cause a chip. |
| 3/5 | Moderate | Projections, veins, and thin sections are vulnerable; a fall may split the specimen. |
| 4/5 | High | Edges or internal structures are easily damaged; stable support and gentle handling are important. |
| 5/5 | Very high | A substantial risk of sharp fragments, separated layers, or the breakdown of a weak specimen. |

The drop scenarios describe a small stone falling from a hand or a table. They are qualitative predictions: the outcome cannot be guaranteed without examining the specimen.

### Care Demand Scale

| Score | Level | Typical Routine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1/5 | Minimal | No daily tasks; a brief cleaning and inspection once a month. |
| 2/5 | Low | Occasionally remove marks or moisture; inspect the surface and stand monthly. |
| 3/5 | Moderate | Watch for splashes, stains, or crumbling; adapt cleaning to the specimen's structure. |
| 4/5 | High | Choose the location and cleaning method carefully; inspect frequently when handling. |
| 5/5 | Very high | An exceptionally weak specimen requiring protected storage and minimal movement. |

A higher care rating does not imply mandatory daily work. It indicates how carefully the storage conditions and handling should be chosen.

## Basalt

**Common name:** basalt.  
**Rock type:** extrusive igneous rock.  
**Character archetype:** quiet protector.  
**Fragility:** 2/5.  
**Care demand:** 1/5.

### Geological Profile

Basalt forms when lava solidifies. Cooling that is rapid on a geological timescale usually produces a fine-grained texture. It is one of the most widespread volcanic rocks. Typical minerals include plagioclase and pyroxenes; some varieties also contain olivine. [1]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** usually dense and fine-grained; individual crystals may be difficult to distinguish without magnification.
- **Surface:** matte or slightly lustrous on a fresh fracture; water-worn specimens can be smooth.
- **Feel in the hand:** a compact, dense piece feels substantial for its size.
- **Porosity:** basalt occurs both as solid, massive rock and as vesicular rock containing cavities left by gas bubbles. Their responses to wetting differ.
- **Resistance to damage:** an intact, massive specimen tolerates ordinary handling well; porous edges, thin projections, and old cracks are more vulnerable.

### Typical Colors

Black, charcoal gray, graphite, and occasionally dark gray-green. A weathered surface may become brown or rusty. Cavities and pale mineral fillings can create spots against the dark background.

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** ordinary indoor sunlight generally causes no noticeable change to basalt. A black stone on a hot windowsill can become quite warm, which is worth considering when choosing its location.

**Moisture:** a dense specimen generally tolerates brief wetting. Water and dirt may remain inside a vesicular specimen, so it should be dried after cleaning. Continuous immersion is neither necessary nor beneficial for its care.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Desk:** a stable, substantial piece beside a monitor or notebook; its restrained appearance pairs well with equipment.
- **Bathroom:** a broad shelf away from direct water flow, with a separate stand. A dense specimen that does not shed particles is the best candidate.
- **Dark corner:** an open alcove with a little side lighting to emphasize the texture.
- **Windowsill:** acceptable if the stone is away from the edge and is not sitting in persistent condensation.

### Psychological Vibe

Basalt is the neighbor who never demands conversation but is always there. Its personality resembles the sea at night: very little seems to happen on the surface, yet a sense of scale remains. In a domestic story, it represents steadiness and the right to quietly occupy one's own space.

It likes a broad stand and a permanent address. Daily rearrangements receive a silent look that says, “We already found a perfectly good spot.”

### Daily Care

No daily care is required. If the stone gets wet, remove water from the stand and let the surface dry. Remove visible dirt as it appears.

### Monthly Care

1. Remove dust with a soft brush or cloth.
2. If necessary, wipe with a slightly damp cloth and dry.
3. Check projections for new chips and make sure the stand does not wobble.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

An intact piece often survives a fall onto carpet. An impact against tile may chip an edge or open an existing crack. Vesicular basalt generally requires more careful handling. Its weight also puts the surface it lands on at risk.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, offer basalt five minutes of silence. Do not expect a reply: it considers shared silence an excellent form of companionship.

## Granite

**Common name:** granite.  
**Rock type:** intrusive igneous rock.  
**Character archetype:** keeper of order.  
**Fragility:** 2/5.  
**Care demand:** 1/5.

### Geological Profile

Granite crystallizes from magma deep within Earth's crust. Slow cooling allows easily visible crystals to grow. It typically contains quartz, feldspars, and small amounts of dark minerals such as mica. [2]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** granular, usually with a visible mosaic of intergrown minerals.
- **Surface:** a natural fracture is rough; polishing strengthens the pattern and adds shine.
- **Feel in the hand:** heavy and substantial; a large specimen needs a reliable shelf.
- **Hardness:** individual grains resist scratching differently. Quartz is harder than many neighboring minerals; the entire rock does not have one exact hardness.
- **Porosity:** often low, but the stone should not be considered completely impermeable to water or oils.

### Typical Colors

Light gray, white-gray, pink, reddish, and beige. Pink and red tones are often associated with feldspars, while dark minerals create speckles. In the stone trade, “black granite” may refer to other rock types, so an almost uniformly black specimen is not necessarily granite.

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** generally tolerates indoor lighting and sunshine on a windowsill. For painted, resin-treated, or varnished pieces, the coating's durability must be considered separately.

**Moisture:** brief wetting usually causes no problems for an intact specimen. Liquid can enter pores and cracks, while oil may leave stains. After damp cleaning, dry the surface and base.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Windowsill:** a convenient location for a sturdy specimen; daylight helps reveal individual grains.
- **Entryway:** a broad, stable shelf where the stone looks like a permanent guardian of the entrance.
- **Study:** beside books, folders, or wooden objects.
- **Bathroom:** a separate, dry shelf away from cosmetic oils and aggressive cleaning products.

### Psychological Vibe

Granite is the character who writes everything down, labels the spare key, and plans a trip in advance. It does not fuss or resent infrequent attention. Its colorful grains soften its serious personality: inside this organizer lives a small community of very different neighbors.

It likes a predictable household. When moving, its first concern is whether the new shelf can support its firmly held views.

### Daily Care

Nothing is mandatory. If coffee, oil, or cosmetics spill nearby, remove the contamination promptly instead of allowing it to soak in.

### Monthly Care

1. Wipe away dust with a soft cloth.
2. If washing is needed, use a little water or a suitable neutral cleaner, remove any residue, and dry. These basic cleaning principles are adapted for a small specimen. [11]
3. Inspect the corners and clean the space beneath the stone.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

A substantial piece of granite is generally sturdier than a thin slab of the same material. A fall onto a hard floor may cause a chip, particularly at a corner. A specimen with an old crack may split along it. The hardness of its quartz grains does not make the whole rock impact-proof.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, conduct an “inventory”: confirm that granite is still in place and that its most attractive grains face the room. It appreciates a brief inspection report with no unnecessary meetings.

## Obsidian

**Common name:** obsidian.  
**Rock type:** volcanic glass.  
**Character archetype:** dramatic introvert.  
**Fragility:** 5/5.  
**Care demand:** 2/5.

### Geological Profile

Obsidian is natural volcanic glass. As the melt cools, crystals fail to develop the coarse crystalline structure found in many other rocks. Its characteristic conchoidal fracture produces smooth, curved broken surfaces. These fractures can create extremely sharp edges. [3, 4]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** predominantly glassy; inclusions, bands, and small crystalline formations may occur.
- **Surface:** smooth and shiny on a fresh fracture; an untreated piece is not necessarily smooth around its entire perimeter.
- **Feel in the hand:** dense and cool to the touch at room temperature; thin edges require caution.
- **Hardness:** approximately 5–5.5 on the Mohs scale, which describes resistance to scratching rather than falling. [3]
- **Main vulnerability:** brittle breakage and sharp chips; a thin edge is much more sensitive than the solid center.

### Typical Colors

Black, smoky brown, and reddish brown. Some varieties have snowflake patterns, bands, or golden, silvery, and rainbow reflections. Not every specimen displays these effects: many simply look like deep black glass. [3]

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** ordinary light is suitable for examination and display. For home storage, choosing a spot without strong overheating followed by abrupt cooling is sensible. This is a practical precaution for a brittle, glassy specimen, rather than a claim that sunlight will inevitably crack it.

**Moisture:** brief contact with water is usually tolerated well. Remove droplets after cleaning to prevent visible marks. In a bathroom, falling onto tile is a more significant concern than humidity alone.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Dark corner:** a protected alcove with soft side lighting. The contrast makes its shine more expressive.
- **Bedroom:** a stable shelf or display case where the stone is rarely handled.
- **Study:** a separate soft pad, without contact with other hard specimens.
- **Windowsill:** possible, although a broad interior shelf is usually more convenient and secure than a position near the window's edge.

### Psychological Vibe

Obsidian sits in the corner wearing black, reflects half the room, and pretends it accidentally became the most striking object in the interior. Its vibe is a nighttime stage, subdued lighting, and a conversation after midnight.

It values personal space: it enjoys being admired, while being passed from hand to hand feels overly familiar. It may look severe, but its main need is simple: set it down carefully and let it shine.

### Daily Care

No mandatory cleaning. Fingerprints on a smooth surface can be removed with a soft, clean cloth. Do not run cloth or fingers along an untreated sharp edge.

### Monthly Care

1. Remove dust with a soft brush or a cloth free of abrasive particles.
2. If necessary, slightly dampen the cloth and wipe the surface dry afterward.
3. Inspect the edges for chips without touching sharp areas.
4. Clean the pad and make sure the stone cannot slide.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

A fall onto tile or concrete can produce a major fracture and extremely sharp fragments. Even a small impact may damage a thin projection. After a fall, inspect the stone and the surrounding area before picking it up by a sound, safe section. A rounded, finished specimen is more convenient for frequent tactile interaction.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, give obsidian an evening of side lighting. Let it reflect a lamp while pretending to be an album cover. One compliment is allowed: a second would become a scheduled expectation.

## Pumice

**Common name:** pumice.  
**Rock type:** porous volcanic rock, predominantly glassy.  
**Character archetype:** lighthearted dreamer.  
**Fragility:** 4/5.  
**Care demand:** 3/5.

### Geological Profile

Pumice forms when gas-rich volcanic melt foams and solidifies. Numerous bubbles leave a network of cavities separated by thin walls. This porosity makes it unusually light. Some pieces can float while their overall density remains lower than that of water. Floating is not guaranteed for every specimen and may change as it becomes waterlogged. [1, 5]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** sponge-like and vesicular, sometimes with elongated pores.
- **Surface:** rough and abrasive; it can scratch a softer object.
- **Feel in the hand:** unexpectedly light compared with a dense stone of the same size.
- **Porosity:** very high; dust can collect in the recesses.
- **Strength:** thin walls between pores and exposed projections may break or crumble. The hardness of the wall material does not make the entire porous structure sturdy.

### Typical Colors

White, cream, light gray, and beige; sometimes darker gray or brownish tones. The surface may display uneven patches and weathering marks.

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** ordinary light is generally not a problem. Its pale texture is easy to appreciate under diffuse lighting.

**Moisture:** water can enter accessible pores; absorption and drying rates depend on the specimen. A collection piece is best kept out of permanently wet storage. After rinsing, let it dry in a ventilated place until the underside is dry as well.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Bright, dry shelf:** somewhere the bubbles are easy to see and moisture does not accumulate.
- **Windowsill:** indoors, on a tray, without persistent condensation.
- **Bathroom:** only a dry, ventilated area. A collection specimen is best kept separate from pumice used for personal care.
- **Beside plants:** an attractive pairing, provided water and soil from the pot do not reach the stone.

### Psychological Vibe

Pumice is the character who arrives at a serious meeting with plans for a house made of clouds. Its presence is light, slightly absurd, and friendly. It suggests that a large volume need not mean a heavy weight, and that internal spaces can be part of the design.

It likes room to breathe and dislikes heavier neighbors being stacked on top. During a move, it occupies half a box and still weighs less than most of the other passengers.

### Daily Care

Nothing is mandatory in a dry location. After accidental wetting, empty water from the tray and let the stone dry. Remove any loose crumbs without rubbing them against furniture.

### Monthly Care

1. Gently clean the surface with a soft brush over a tray.
2. Inspect projections for increased crumbling.
3. Rinse only when necessary and only if the specimen is sufficiently sound.
4. After rinsing, dry completely; do not enclose a damp stone in a sealed box.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

Because it is light, pumice may survive a fall without a dramatic split, but individual pore walls and projections can break off. A particularly friable specimen may shed material under strong squeezing or rubbing. Do not use a collection piece as a support for heavy objects.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, announce a “day of airy plans”: place pumice on a clean tray and tell it about one idea without committing to carrying it out. It likes thoughts that have a few open spaces of their own.

## Limestone

**Common name:** limestone.  
**Rock type:** sedimentary carbonate rock.  
**Character archetype:** household archivist.  
**Fragility:** 3/5.  
**Care demand:** 3/5.

### Geological Profile

Limestone consists primarily of calcium carbonate, usually in the form of calcite. It can form from accumulated remains of organisms or through the precipitation of carbonate material. Many varieties preserve shells, skeletal fragments, or other traces of ancient life. [6, 7]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** ranges from dense and fine-grained to porous, shell-rich, or friable.
- **Surface:** often matte; it can be smooth, granular, or marked by clearly visible fossils.
- **Hardness:** calcite scratches relatively easily. Impurities and structure alter the behavior of the whole stone.
- **Porosity:** highly variable; two limestone specimens can absorb water very differently.
- **Chemical sensitivity:** the carbonate material reacts with acids. Vinegar and lemon juice can damage the surface. [7]

### Typical Colors

White, milky white, cream, sandy beige, and light gray. Impurities can produce brown, yellowish, or dark tones. Shells and fossils sometimes stand out through color or relief.

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** ordinary daylight is generally acceptable. A windowsill is suitable if it stays dry and does not subject the specimen to persistent changes in moisture.

**Moisture:** a dense specimen tolerates brief wetting better than a friable, porous one. Prolonged wet storage, contaminated water, and repeated wetting cycles can be problematic for sensitive specimens. A drop of ordinary water does not mean immediate destruction; structure and contact duration matter much more.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Dry living-room shelf:** particularly good for a shell-bearing specimen worth examining closely.
- **Windowsill without condensation:** a broad surface with a stand.
- **Dark corner:** a dry alcove among books or ceramics; extra light serves the viewer rather than the stone.
- **Bathroom:** a dry shelf is acceptable, but a wet zone and nearby acidic cleaning products are inconvenient.

### Psychological Vibe

Limestone is a warmhearted keeper of other people's stories. It likes old books, pale ceramics, and unhurried attention. Visible shells make it a character that carries the family archive on its surface.

It does not demand daily conversations. Its favorite form of closeness is sharing ordinary life and occasionally having someone notice a new little pattern.

### Daily Care

No daily tasks are needed in a dry location. Blot spilled liquid promptly. In particular, do not leave juice, vinegar, coffee, or cosmetics on the stone.

### Monthly Care

1. Remove dust with a soft brush.
2. If necessary, use a slightly damp cloth on a dense specimen; favor dry cleaning for a friable one.
3. Inspect the surface for new powdery areas and check that the stand is dry.
4. Do not apply oil or varnish simply to intensify the color: it may leave stains or change the natural surface.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

A fall may chip a corner, split the specimen along a crack, or damage a projecting fossil. Friable, shell-rich limestone can be considerably more fragile than a dense variety. Position valuable details so they do not press against the stand.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, host an “archive evening”: examine one shell or vein and invent a little story for it. Limestone does not object to fiction, but it likes being allowed to finish its story, even if only in your imagination.

## Sandstone

**Common name:** sandstone.  
**Rock type:** clastic sedimentary rock.  
**Character archetype:** unhurried traveler.  
**Fragility:** 3/5; 4/5 for a friable specimen.  
**Care demand:** 2/5; 3/5 for a specimen that sheds grains.

### Geological Profile

Sandstone consists of sand-sized grains held together by natural cement. The grains are often quartz, but may include feldspars and fragments of other rocks. The cement and degree of consolidation determine whether a piece is very sturdy or readily releases individual grains. [6]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** granular; layers, bands, and differences in grain size are often visible.
- **Surface:** generally rough, sometimes resembling very tightly packed sand.
- **Strength:** hard quartz grains may be bound by relatively weak cement. “Contains quartz” therefore does not mean “impossible to crumble.”
- **Porosity:** can be substantial, but differs greatly between varieties.
- **Handling traces:** a friable specimen may leave individual grains behind after being moved. Strong rubbing can increase shedding.

### Typical Colors

Sandy, cream, beige, ocher, yellow, red, reddish brown, and gray. Iron-bearing compounds often produce warm red and ocher tones. A layered pattern may resemble dunes or a cross-section through a landscape. [6]

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** ordinary light is generally suitable. On a bright windowsill, the main concerns are stable placement and any condensation.

**Moisture:** a strong, well-cemented specimen may tolerate wetting, while a friable one may weaken or lose grains. Carbonate cement is also sensitive to acids. For an unfamiliar collection specimen, dry storage and minimal damp cleaning are preferable.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Dry windowsill:** warm daylight emphasizes sandy tones.
- **Shelf beside wood and ceramics:** its texture pairs well with matte materials.
- **Dark corner:** acceptable if dry; a small lamp helps reveal the pattern.
- **Bathroom:** inconvenient for an unidentified or friable specimen. If used, choose a dry shelf away from splashes.

### Psychological Vibe

Sandstone is a traveler who never seems to be in a hurry. It carries sunshine, old walls, a dry path, and memories of the road. It creates the mood of a place where you can stop checking the time.

Its particular quirk is leaving a little sand behind, as though it is still coming home from the beach. The neighbors are happy to overlook this once it has a tray of its own.

### Daily Care

Daily cleaning is unnecessary. If the stone sheds grains, remove them from its tray without rubbing the specimen itself. Do not sweep the stone and loose grains together across varnished furniture.

### Monthly Care

1. Remove dust with a very soft brush and little pressure.
2. Check whether grain shedding has increased noticeably.
3. Clean the tray and inspect the layered edges.
4. Use damp cleaning only on a sufficiently sturdy specimen; avoid soaking a friable one.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

An impact may chip edges, separate a layer, or break down weak cement. Well-cemented sandstone can be quite strong, so one rating cannot describe every specimen. Carpet cushions a fall, but a projecting corner may still be damaged.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, turn the stone to reveal another part of its layers and declare it a new route. Sandstone considers this a proper journey, even though no ticket or vacation was required.

## Shale

**Common name:** shale, a fissile clay-rich sedimentary rock.  
**Rock type:** fine-grained sedimentary rock.  
**Character archetype:** keeper of fragile pages.  
**Fragility:** 5/5 for a thin or weathered specimen.  
**Care demand:** 4/5.

### Geological Profile

Shale forms from compacted fine sediments containing clay- and silt-sized particles. Its characteristic feature is the ability to split along thin layers. Mechanical behavior depends on the direction of bedding; this variation has been investigated in real specimens. [6, 8]

**Shale and slate are different rocks.** Shale is a sedimentary rock; slate is a metamorphic rock. This distinction matters for care: a weak shale specimen should not be assumed to have the water resistance of good-quality roofing slate.

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** very fine-grained and layered; individual particles are usually invisible without magnification.
- **Surface:** predominantly matte, sometimes dusty, with thin flakes along the edges.
- **Strength:** a dense, fresh piece may be sturdy, while a weathered one may separate into small sheets.
- **Directional behavior:** impacts and loads across the layers act differently from those along them.
- **Vulnerability:** thin projections and raised flakes can detach easily through rubbing or attempts to clean them with a stiff brush.

### Typical Colors

Gray, dark gray, almost black, brown, greenish, or reddish. Color depends on minerals, organic matter, and oxidation. A bedding surface may sometimes preserve an impression of an ancient organism.

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** indoor light is usually not the main risk. A fragile specimen is better kept in a stable location without strong heating or constant changes in conditions.

**Moisture:** many clay-rich shales are sensitive to wetting and subsequent drying. Weakening, separation into layers, and crumbling are possible; the extent depends on composition and condition. Until the particular specimen's properties are known, avoid washing it under a tap or leaving it in a damp area.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Dry, dark corner:** a quiet alcove with stable conditions.
- **Enclosed display case:** a dry, clean specimen supported and protected from frequent handling and dust accumulation.
- **Bookshelf:** in a small tray, separate from books so fine particles cannot scatter between their pages.
- **Bathroom or damp windowsill:** unsuitable for a typical fragile collection specimen.

### Psychological Vibe

Shale keeps letters in a box and remembers exactly where a dried leaf once rested between the pages. Its vibe is an archive, a subdued room, and delicate memories of earlier states.

It prefers gentle attention. It enjoys having its layered patterns admired, but regards an attempt to “open one more page” as a breach of personal boundaries.

### Daily Care

Avoid unnecessary handling. If flakes detach, gently remove them from the surrounding area or keep them beside the specimen as parts of it. Frequent wiping may do more harm than the dust itself.

### Monthly Care

1. Inspect without moving the stone, or lift it together with its tray.
2. If the surface is sufficiently sound, very gently remove loose dust with a soft brush.
3. Check for newly raised layers and signs of dampness.
4. Do not pull away weakened flakes or use household glue simply to make the specimen look tidier.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

A fall may break a plate or separate a weathered piece into several layers. Even a fall onto a soft surface can damage a thin edge. Support the entire underside when carrying the specimen instead of holding a single projecting sheet.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, observe a “day of unread letters”: open nothing, and simply make sure the archive is dry and peaceful. Shale especially appreciates care that does not require it to reveal its inner world.

## Marble

**Common name:** marble.  
**Rock type:** metamorphic carbonate rock.  
**Character archetype:** gentle aesthete.  
**Fragility:** 3/5.  
**Care demand:** 3/5.

### Geological Profile

Marble forms through the metamorphism of carbonate rocks, primarily limestone, as well as dolomitic rocks. Mineral grains recrystallize. Unlike slate, typical marble does not regularly split into thin sheets. [9, 10]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** crystalline; a broken surface may show a sugary sparkle from individual grains.
- **Surface:** an untreated piece can be rough; polishing produces smoothness and a pronounced shine.
- **Hardness:** calcite-rich varieties scratch relatively easily.
- **Optical effect:** thin edges of some pale varieties are slightly translucent.
- **Vulnerability:** veins, cracks, and thin sections can become fracture sites; the carbonate material is sensitive to acids. [7]

### Typical Colors

White, milky white, cream, gray, and pink; dark and colored varieties also occur. Veins can be gray, black, golden, green, or reddish. Not every green decorative stone sold as marble is geologically marble: commercial names are broader than scientific classifications.

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** ordinary lighting is generally acceptable. For resin-treated, painted, or varnished pieces, possible changes to the coating should be considered separately.

**Moisture:** brief wetting does not usually mean damage. However, absorption through pores and microcracks can lead to staining, and particular impurities can affect the result. For a small collection specimen, a dry shelf is easier to manage than a constantly wet area.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Bedroom:** a dry shelf with soft lighting and a separate stand.
- **Living room:** beside glass, fabric, and plain objects that allow the veins to remain clearly visible.
- **Windowsill:** acceptable without condensation; a substantial specimen must stand securely.
- **Bathroom:** a dry decorative alcove away from cosmetics and acidic limescale removers.

### Psychological Vibe

Marble is an aesthete who needs few possessions but likes each one to be placed beautifully. Its mood is a slow evening, clean fabric, a pale wall, and a thoughtfully arranged space.

It appreciates attention to detail, but its personality need not be snobbish: this pet enjoys having its one unusual vein noticed rather than being expected to remain perfectly white.

### Daily Care

Nothing is mandatory in a dry location. Blot droplets, juice, coffee, oils, and cosmetics promptly. Do not rub a polished surface with a cloth containing sand or other hard particles.

### Monthly Care

1. Remove dust with a soft, clean cloth or brush.
2. If necessary, wipe with a slightly damp cloth; use a suitable neutral cleaner for dirt.
3. Remove cleaner residue and dry the surface.
4. Inspect veins and edges. A small specimen does not automatically need monthly polishing or regular sealing.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

A fall onto tile can cause chipping or splitting, especially in a thin plate or a cracked specimen. An impact on a vein does not necessarily break the stone, but such areas are worth checking. A scratch in the polish is a different kind of damage from a structural fracture.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, host a “portrait evening”: gently clean the stone and find lighting that makes one vein resemble a river. Marble considers this excellent care and requires neither a new interior nor a forty-photo session.

## Gneiss

**Common name:** gneiss.  
**Rock type:** metamorphic rock with pronounced mineral banding.  
**Character archetype:** calm storyteller.  
**Fragility:** 2/5; 3/5 for a cracked or weakened specimen.  
**Care demand:** 1/5.

### Geological Profile

Gneiss forms through the metamorphism of various parent rocks; granite is one possible precursor. It typically displays minerals separated into light and dark bands, which may be straight, wavy, or folded. It belongs to the group of rocks with a directional fabric. [9, 10]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** crystalline, often medium- or coarse-grained, with mineral banding.
- **Composition:** may include quartz, feldspars, micas, and other minerals; the combination depends on the variety.
- **Feel in the hand:** generally dense and substantial.
- **Surface:** rough on a natural fracture, smooth when water-worn or finished.
- **Directional behavior:** properties may depend on the orientation of its fabric. Gneiss bands should not automatically be treated as easily separated sheets like those of weak shale.

### Typical Colors

Combinations of white, gray, pink, and black; sometimes brown or greenish areas. The alternating bands are usually more distinctive than any single color. Some varieties contain large, pale grains resembling eyes within the darker pattern.

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** ordinary lighting and indoor sunlight are generally suitable. Daylight from the side makes the bands particularly easy to read.

**Moisture:** an intact, dense specimen usually tolerates brief wetting. Cracks, weathered areas, and weakened mineral zones call for greater care. Dry the base and recesses after cleaning.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Desk:** a clearly patterned stone to examine during a pause.
- **Windowsill:** a broad, stable surface; side lighting emphasizes the bands.
- **Bookshelf:** pairs well with objects of different textures and ages.
- **Bathroom:** a dry shelf is possible for an intact specimen, away from direct water flow and slippery edges.

### Psychological Vibe

Gneiss has a long biography, has seen many changes, and can speak calmly about them. Its bands resemble several periods of life brought together in one shape. Its mood is steadiness without the obligation to remain the same forever.

It is friendlier than stern granite and less talkative than its rich pattern might suggest. It gets along with different neighbors, provided everyone has enough room.

### Daily Care

No daily tasks. If droplets appear, remove water from the stand. If the stone has been handled with dirty hands, clean visible marks as needed.

### Monthly Care

1. Remove dust with a soft brush or cloth.
2. If necessary, wipe with a slightly damp cloth and dry.
3. Check banded edges and old cracks for new changes.
4. Make sure the heavy specimen stands securely and does not press a sharp projection into the shelf.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

A substantial, intact piece is usually sturdy. A fall onto a hard floor can cause a chip or a fracture along a weakened zone. Thin plates and weathered specimens are more vulnerable. An attractive striped pattern does not, by itself, establish either fragility or impact resistance.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, discover a new line in the pattern and give it a chapter title. Gneiss accepts both “The Big Move” and “The Week It Finally Became Quiet.” It respects short chapters just as much as long ones.

## Slate

**Common name:** slate; suitable varieties are also known as roofing slate.  
**Rock type:** fine-grained metamorphic rock.  
**Character archetype:** calm organizer.  
**Fragility:** 3/5; 4/5 for a thin plate.  
**Care demand:** 2/5.

### Geological Profile

Slate generally develops through low-grade metamorphism of fine-grained, clay-rich rocks, including shale. It has slaty cleavage: the ability to split along closely spaced planes into flat sheets. These planes do not necessarily coincide with the original sedimentary bedding. [9, 10]

### Physical Properties

- **Structure:** fine-grained and dense, with distinct cleavage planes.
- **Surface:** usually matte, relatively smooth along a plane, or marked by fine relief.
- **Shape:** often flat and angular; thickness strongly affects the risk of breakage.
- **Strength:** high-quality, dense slate can be durable, but a thin edge may chip.
- **Vulnerability:** layered edges, existing cracks, and attempts to bend an unsupported plate.

### Typical Colors

Dark gray, graphite, black, gray-green, greenish, purple, and brown. Rust-colored patches and color transitions may reflect impurities and surface changes.

### Sunlight and Moisture Resistance

**Sunlight:** ordinary light is generally tolerated well. A dark plate may become noticeably warm in sunshine.

**Moisture:** high-quality, dense varieties are used in conditions exposed to rain, but collection specimens do not all have the same durability. A piece with weakened edges or flaking areas is better kept dry. After damp cleaning, dry both sides.

### Ideal Places at Home

- **Study:** on a level stand that supports the whole plate.
- **Bathroom:** a dry, stable shelf away from direct splashes, for a dense, sound specimen.
- **Dark corner:** a simple alcove with a contrasting background.
- **Windowsill:** possible if the plate rests securely and does not project beyond the edge.

### Psychological Vibe

Slate is the organizer who leaves exactly as many objects on the desk as are needed. Its mood is a clear surface, subdued colors, and quiet order. Compared with the more fragile archivist shale, it seems composed and ready for everyday domestic life.

It likes having a place of its own and reliable support. It may silently disapprove of a stack of objects on top, especially if appointed to the role of coaster without a discussion.

### Daily Care

No mandatory cleaning. In a bathroom, wipe away accidental water and make sure no puddle remains beneath the plate. Do not lift a thin specimen by a distant corner.

### Monthly Care

1. Remove dust with a soft cloth or brush.
2. If needed, clean gently with a damp cloth and dry both sides.
3. Inspect the edges for newly separating flakes.
4. Check the support is level; do not leave a thin plate bridging a gap between two contact points.

### Fragility and Drop Behavior

A flat piece can break across its width when dropped onto an edge, or lose a thin surface layer. A corner impact often produces a small chip. A thick, dense specimen is generally sturdier than a thin one; even a water-resistant variety remains brittle under bending and impact.

### Fictional Care Ritual

Once a month, hold an “evening of clear surfaces”: remove excess clutter beside the stone and leave it one clean plane. Slate considers this a proper household occasion and asks you not to turn it into a project to organize the entire apartment.

## General Care Rules

### Everyday Essentials

- Stones do not need watering or sunbathing. Daily care usually means responding to accidental contamination.
- Match the stand to the weight and shape: a heavy piece needs a sturdy shelf, a thin plate needs support from beneath, and a shedding specimen needs a tray.
- If a stone is already cracked or crumbling, its condition matters more than a general description of the rock type.
- A dry, dark corner does not require a special lamp. Add lighting so that people can examine the stone.

### Cleaning Principles

For an unidentified, untreated specimen, start with gentle dry cleaning. Use damp cleaning only if its structure allows it. For suitable dense surfaces, favor a soft cloth and a neutral cleaner; remove or rinse away residues, then dry. [7]

Acidic cleaners are particularly unsuitable for limestone and marble. Abrasive cleaning can damage softer minerals and polish. Protective sealing is not a mandatory monthly ritual: the need depends on the object and its conditions of use. [7]

### Handling Principles

- Obsidian with sharp edges is more convenient as a display specimen than as something to pass repeatedly from hand to hand.
- Carry shale, thin slate, and friable sandstone with the entire underside supported.
- Do not rub pumice or shedding specimens against furniture: individual particles may scratch it.
- After a fall, inspect the stone and the surrounding area before touching it again.

### Monthly Check

An ordinary inspection takes a few minutes: remove accessible dust, examine the edges, and check that the stand is dry and stable. New crumbs, flaking, or a damp patch beneath the stone are reasons to adjust its location or reduce handling.
